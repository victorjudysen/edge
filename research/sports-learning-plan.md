# Edge Sports Learning Plan

- Status: Proposed learning track
- Last updated: 2026-09-07
- Purpose: Build sport and market literacy before model-led betting research

## Why this exists

Edge is not only a modelling project. Victor should understand what is being
modelled before trusting a probability, price, or decision label.

Learning a sport and proving a betting edge are different tasks:

- **Sport literacy** means understanding the rules, event structure, strategy,
  player roles, and sources of randomness.
- **Market literacy** means understanding odds, bookmaker margin, market types,
  timing, and settlement rules.
- **Research evidence** means testing whether a model adds information after
  those uncertainties are measured.

Knowing a sport well does not guarantee profitable betting. A model can also be
accurate about a sport and still offer poor prices.

## Learning sequence

The recommended order is:

1. Tennis — one player versus one player and a relatively small number of core
   outcomes.
2. Basketball — more possessions and data, but more complex rotation and pace
   effects.
3. Hockey — lower scoring, larger event randomness, and important goalie and
   special-teams information.

Each sport follows the same learning loop:

1. Learn the game.
2. Learn the market.
3. Watch and explain matches without predicting them.
4. Make recorded paper forecasts.
5. Compare forecasts with outcomes and closing prices.
6. Only then consider whether a formal research question is justified.

## Tennis track

### Game fundamentals

- Points form games; games form sets; sets form a match.
- A player usually needs two sets in a best-of-three match, while some major
  events use best-of-five for men’s singles.
- A serve starts each point. Holding serve and breaking the opponent’s serve are
  central events.
- Tiebreak rules and deciding-set rules can differ by event.
- Surface changes how the ball moves and how valuable serving, returning, and
  movement are.

### What to observe

- Serve quality and second-serve weakness.
- Return quality and break-point creation.
- Rally tolerance and unforced errors.
- Surface-specific performance.
- Fatigue, travel, rest, injuries, and the tournament schedule.
- Matchup style: for example, a strong returner against a player dependent on
  short points.

### Common markets

- Match winner: which player wins the match.
- Set winner: which player wins a particular set.
- Game handicap: one player must win by more than a specified game margin.
- Total games: whether the match has more or fewer than a specified number of
  games.

### Important traps

- A favourite can win the match but fail a game handicap.
- A retirement can make an apparent forecast result ambiguous.
- Current ranking is not the same as current ability on a particular surface.
- A small number of matches can make recent form look stronger than it is.
- Pre-match information must not include a later withdrawal or injury update.

## Basketball track

### Game fundamentals

- Teams alternate possessions, trying to create high-quality shots and prevent
  the opponent from doing so.
- Points are scored mainly through two-point shots, three-point shots, and free
  throws.
- The game is divided into quarters, and overtime can change settlement rules.
- Rotations matter: the players on the court can change the team’s attack,
  defence, and pace substantially.

### What to observe

- Pace: how many possessions a team creates.
- Offensive efficiency: points scored per possession.
- Defensive efficiency: points allowed per possession.
- Shot profile, rebounding, turnovers, and free throws.
- Injuries, minutes restrictions, rest, travel, and back-to-back games.
- Rotation changes and whether a team’s recent results came against unusually
  strong or weak opponents.

### Common markets

- Moneyline: which team wins.
- Point spread: whether a team wins or loses by more than the quoted margin.
- Total points: whether the combined score is above or below the quoted total.
- Team totals and player props, which should remain outside the first research
  phase.

### Important traps

- A team can win while failing to cover the spread.
- A late injury can change the true price quickly.
- High-scoring recent games may reflect pace rather than improved shooting.
- Garbage-time scoring can distort simple averages.
- Overtime treatment must be checked for every market.

## Hockey track

### Game fundamentals

- Teams play three periods, with line changes and special teams affecting play.
- Goals are relatively scarce, so one deflection, penalty, or goalie mistake can
  have a large effect on the result.
- Power plays occur when a team has a numerical advantage after a penalty.
- Overtime and shootouts can be settled differently across markets and leagues.

### What to observe

- Shot volume and shot quality rather than goals alone.
- Goalie identity, recent workload, and confirmed starter status.
- Power-play and penalty-killing performance.
- Five-on-five territorial play and scoring chances.
- Line combinations, injuries, travel, rest, and schedule density.

### Common markets

- Moneyline: which team wins, sometimes including overtime and shootouts.
- Regulation result: which team leads after three periods.
- Puck line: a goal-based handicap.
- Total goals: whether the combined score is above or below the quoted total.

### Important traps

- “Win” and “win in regulation” are different events.
- A strong team can lose because hockey has few scoring events.
- Goalie news can matter more than a team’s season average.
- Empty-net goals can affect totals and margins late in games.
- Shootout results are noisy and should not be treated like ordinary regulation
  performance.

## Paper-trading protocol

Paper trading means recording a hypothetical decision as if it were made, while
keeping money out of the process. It is an observation and learning exercise,
not proof of profitability.

For every paper forecast, record:

- sport, league, event, and market;
- exact prediction timestamp;
- available price and bookmaker or source;
- estimated probability and how it was formed;
- implied probability after noting bookmaker margin;
- reason for the forecast;
- uncertainty and information that was unavailable;
- eventual result and closing price;
- whether the decision would have been BET, WATCH, or SKIP under the current
  rules.

Do not change the forecast after the prediction timestamp. Later information can
be recorded separately as an explanation of what changed, not inserted into the
original decision.

## Learning milestones

Before a sport enters formal betting-market research, Victor should be able to:

- explain the basic scoring and settlement rules without reference material;
- explain the difference between moneyline, handicap/spread, and totals;
- calculate implied probability from decimal odds;
- explain bookmaker margin in plain language;
- identify the most important sport-specific uncertainties;
- describe why a likely winner may still be a poor price;
- complete at least one reviewable paper-forecast sample without changing
  timestamps or selection rules after the fact.

These milestones indicate understanding, not betting readiness or financial
permission.

## Safety boundary

This learning plan does not recommend real-money bets, stake sizes, bookmakers,
or gambling accounts. Edge should use paper forecasts until the sport, market,
data rights, and research evidence have been reviewed separately.
