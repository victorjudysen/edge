# Tennis Lesson 01 — How the Match and Market Work

- Status: Learning material
- Last updated: 2026-09-07
- Audience: Beginner tennis and tennis-market learner

## The basic idea

Tennis is a one-versus-one sport. Players do not share a team score: each
player tries to win points, games, and sets before the opponent does.

The first useful distinction is:

- **Match prediction:** who is more likely to win.
- **Market decision:** whether the price is good enough for that probability.

A player can be the likely winner and still be offered at a price that is too
short to justify a bet.

## How scoring is built

### Points make games

The traditional score inside a game is:

`love → 15 → 30 → 40 → game`

If both players reach 40, the score is **deuce**. A player generally needs to
win two points in a row from deuce: an advantage point, then the game-winning
point. Some competitions use alternative deciding-point rules, so the event’s
rules must be checked.

### Games make sets

A player normally wins a set by reaching at least six games and leading by two.
At 6–6, a tiebreak is commonly played, but the exact deciding-set rule can
vary by tournament.

### Sets make matches

Most ATP Tour matches are best-of-three sets: the first player to win two sets
wins the match. Some major events use best-of-five for men’s singles. These
formats must not be mixed casually because they change fatigue, comeback, and
probability patterns.

## The first market to learn: match winner

The match-winner market asks one question:

> Which player will win the match?

There are two outcomes, unlike football’s home/draw/away market. The market
usually displays decimal odds for both players.

Example:

| Player | Decimal odds | Raw implied probability |
|---|---:|---:|
| Player A | 1.50 | 66.7% |
| Player B | 2.80 | 35.7% |

The raw implied probabilities add to 102.4%, not 100%. The extra 2.4% is an
approximation of the bookmaker’s margin, often called the **overround**. It is
the built-in gap that helps the bookmaker cover costs and make a profit.

To create a simple margin-free market estimate, divide each raw probability by
the total:

- Player A: 66.7 ÷ 102.4 ≈ 65.1%
- Player B: 35.7 ÷ 102.4 ≈ 34.9%

This is only one margin-removal method. Edge must compare methods later instead
of assuming one formula is automatically correct.

## Why the favourite is not automatically a good bet

Suppose Edge independently estimates that Player A has a 60% chance of winning.
The fair decimal price is approximately:

`1 ÷ 0.60 = 1.67`

If the available price is 1.40, Player A may still be the most likely winner,
but the price is shorter than the estimated fair price. The forecast and the
price are answering different questions.

The correct learning habit is to write both down:

- estimated probability: 60%;
- available price: 1.40;
- fair price from the estimate: 1.67;
- decision: not determined by probability alone.

## What makes a tennis match difficult to predict

Start by observing these factors:

- serve and return quality;
- surface: hard, clay, or grass;
- player fitness and recent workload;
- travel and rest between matches;
- opponent style and matchup;
- historical performance on the same surface;
- tournament stage and pressure;
- withdrawals, injuries, and retirement risk.

Do not treat a player’s ranking as a complete forecast. Ranking is useful
context, but it may lag current form, surface ability, or physical condition.

## First paper exercise

Choose one completed or upcoming match for observation only. Write down before
the match:

1. Which player appears more likely to win, and why?
2. What are the two available match-winner prices?
3. What raw probabilities do those prices imply?
4. What is the approximate overround?
5. What information is missing or uncertain?
6. Would the correct learning decision be BET, WATCH, or SKIP under the current
   evidence standard?

Do not place money. Do not change the written forecast after the match begins.
Afterward, record the result and ask:

- Was the winner the more likely player?
- Was the price attractive, or did we only pick the likely winner?
- Did a later injury or withdrawal change what was knowable before the match?

One match teaches almost nothing statistically. The exercise is for learning the
game and the market language, not for proving a strategy.

## Lesson takeaway

For every tennis market, separate three questions:

1. Who is more likely to win?
2. What probability does the market imply after accounting for margin?
3. Is the available price worth the uncertainty?

Edge will not treat a correct winner pick as evidence of a betting edge unless
the price, timing, uncertainty, and repeated out-of-sample record also support
that conclusion.
